Chapter 06

Solving it

The coupled system has two properties that make it awkward: it is a saddle-point problem in space, and it is stiff in time. Both have well-understood consequences, and both punish the obvious implementation. This chapter is about the two traps and the standard ways out.

Discretisation

Almost every production THM code discretises displacement with continuous finite elements, because the momentum balance is naturally variational and the boundary conditions are tractions. For pressure and temperature the field splits: finite elements are common in geotechnics, finite volumes in reservoir simulation, because they conserve mass locally on each cell and handle strong heterogeneity in $k$ more gracefully.

Trap 1 — spurious pressure oscillations

In the incompressible, undrained limit — small $\Delta t$, small permeability, small storage — the bottom-right block $\ten{S}+\Delta t\,\ten{H}$ nearly vanishes and the system becomes a pure saddle-point problem, structurally identical to Stokes flow. It then inherits the inf–sup (LBB) condition: equal-order interpolation of displacement and pressure is not stable, and the pressure field decorates itself with checkerboard oscillations near drained boundaries.

This is not a bug you can refine away. Halving the element size while holding $\Delta t$ makes it worse, because the offending dimensionless group is $\Delta t\,c/h^2$: the oscillations appear when the time step is too small for the mesh. Three ways out
  • Satisfy inf–sup. Use Taylor–Hood elements: quadratic displacement, linear pressure. Correct, robust, and the most expensive per node.
  • Stabilise. Add a consistent pressure-Laplacian or polynomial-pressure-projection term scaled by $h^2/(4G)$. Cheap, keeps equal order, and is what most codes actually do.
  • Respect the time-step limit. Vermeer and Verruijt’s criterion $\Delta t \ge h^2/(6c)$ keeps an unstabilised equal-order scheme clean. Simple, but it forbids resolving the early transient — which is often exactly what you wanted.

Trap 2 — solving the blocks separately

Solving the full block system at once — monolithic coupling — is the unconditionally stable choice, but it means writing one solver that owns all the physics. In practice people very often want to reuse an existing flow simulator and an existing geomechanics code, exchanging fields between them. That is a sequential scheme, and whether it converges depends entirely on which quantity you hold fixed while solving each sub-problem.

Raise the coupling strength past $\tau = 1$ and the drained and fixed-strain splits diverge outright; the fixed-stress split keeps converging no matter how strongly coupled the problem is, which is why it has become the default.

Why fixed-stress works

The trick is to solve the flow problem with a storage coefficient that already anticipates the mechanical response. Time stepping and nonlinearity

Start small, then grow

Every THM problem has an initial transient — undrained loading, a heater switch-on — over which the pressure changes by orders of magnitude. Geometric time-step growth from a small first step, with a target on the Newton iteration count, is standard and effective. But respect the equal-order lower bound if you are not stabilised.

Upwind the advection, or pay

The $(\rho c)_f\vec q\cdot\nabla T$ term is hyperbolic. Central differencing it on a grid with cell Péclet number above 2 produces oscillations that look exactly like a physical instability. Upwinding, SUPG, or a flux-limited scheme is not optional in advection-dominated problems.

Lag the weak couplings

Temperature feeds back on the others weakly enough that a T→H→M sequential order, with one pass per step, is usually accurate. Verify it by running one case monolithically — not by assuming.

Nonlinear coefficients need iteration

$k(\phi)$, $\mu(T)$, $k_r(S)$ and any plasticity make the system genuinely nonlinear. Newton with a consistent tangent converges quadratically; a fixed-point update of the coefficients converges linearly and can stall when the retention curve is steep.

Verifying a coupled code

A checklist that catches most implementation errors, in the order that isolates them fastest:

  1. Patch tests on each physics separately — uniform stress, uniform flux, uniform temperature gradient — must be exact to machine precision.
  2. Terzaghi. One-dimensional, analytic, exercises the $\ten{Q}$ blocks and the time integration. If the early-time pressure is not $\sigma_0$ everywhere, the undrained limit is wrong.
  3. Mandel. Two-dimensional, analytic, and the only common benchmark whose answer is non-monotonic. It catches a lagged or transposed coupling block that Terzaghi passes happily.
  4. Booker & Savvidou — a point or spherical heat source in a poroelastic half-space, analytic — brings the thermal blocks in.
  5. A convergence study in both $h$ and $\Delta t$, checking that the observed orders match the scheme. A coupled code that is first-order accurate where it should be second-order usually has a term evaluated at the wrong time level.
  6. Energy and mass audits over the whole domain, every step. Cheap, and they catch boundary-condition sign errors that convergence studies miss.
